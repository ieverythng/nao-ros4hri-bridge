Abstract
Large language models have shown strong potential for instruction understanding and high-
level reasoning in human-robot interaction. Their integration into a robot nevertheless
requires more than a planner call: dialogue state, symbolic grounding, capability descriptions,
execution admission, robot-facing skills, feedback, and user-facing reporting must remain
coherent across a distributed runtime. This thesis presents a modular LLM-based interactive
architecture and evaluates its implementation on the NAO robot.
The system separates dialogue management, semantic routing, symbolic grounding, task
planning, deterministic orchestration, and skill execution. A chatbot interprets each turn
and selects a conversational, knowledge, or execution route. A planner generates structured
plans over a reviewed skill registry, while an orchestrator admits requests, validates plans,
dispatches skills, and returns typed execution feedback. Symbolic knowledge representation
and perception jointly provide compact grounded context without allowing planning-time
state to stand in for execution evidence.
The contribution is an inspectable interactive architecture and a validation method for its
principal boundaries. The validation follows complete user turns together with focused planner,
skill-execution, and controlled failure tests. It examines route correctness, plan validity, entity
and role preservation, action order, symbolic postconditions, failure handling, and user-facing
reporting.
The primary evidence index contains 108 case records from 33 retained structured traces. The
frozen Qwen3-VL configuration produced 64 passes and eight non-passes across 72 records,
while the twenty-two-case end-to-end questionnaire produced twenty passes and two failures.
A separate 191-record matched comparison across three Ollama-hosted models preserved the
environment path for all models but showed variation in composition, grounded selection,
clarification, and closure. Controlled beginning- and middle-stage skill failures were recovered
through versioned replanning, while incomplete plans were rejected before execution.
Keywords:Human-Robot Interaction, ROS 2, Large Language Models, Task Planning, NAO
Robot, Symbolic Grounding, Deterministic Execution, Runtime Validation.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 Language-Guided Robot Interaction and Execution
Human-robot interaction (HRI) requires a robot to connect communication with perception,
task reasoning, and embodied action. Automated planning provides one part of this problem.
Classical languages such as the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) make actions,
preconditions, effects, initial states, and goals explicit (McDermott et al. 1998). Hierarchical
task network methods further describe how high-level tasks can be decomposed into executable
subtasks (Erol et al. 1994; Nau et al. 2003; Georgievski et al. 2014). Language-model-assisted
planning revisits the connection between flexible instructions and grounded action structures
(Liu et al. 2023a; Valmeekam et al. 2023; Ahn et al. 2022).
Large language models (LLMs) can transform user utterances into structured intent payloads,
infer task goals, and propose multi-step action decompositions. This flexibility is useful because
users rarely speak in planner-ready commands. They ask questions, refer to visible people
or objects, change goals, and expect responses that remain consistent with the interaction
history. LLMs are not inherently grounded in the robot’s live state, available skills, or
execution constraints. A linguistically plausible response may select the wrong route, refer to
an unavailable entity, propose an unsupported action, or report success without corresponding
execution evidence.
This thesis addresses that problem through a modular interactive architecture. The system
combines dialogue management, LLM-based semantic interpretation, symbolic grounding, task
planning, deterministic orchestration, robot skills, and feedback-driven user communication.
The language model participates in dialogue and planning, while explicit interfaces, reviewed
skill descriptions, and an orchestrator retain control over execution. The focus is therefore
the complete interaction architecture rather than the planner in isolation.

Five recurring problems define the scope of this thesis:

cannot continue?

Chapter 2
Background and Literature Review
2.1 Human-Robot Interaction
Human-robot interaction (HRI) studies how people and robots communicate, coordinate tasks,
and share physical or social space. In service and social robotics, an interaction cannot be
reduced to command recognition. The robot must associate speech and other social signals
with people, objects, prior turns, and the state of an embodied task. It must also communicate
uncertainty and failure in a manner that is understandable to the user. Surveys of HRI and
socially interactive robots therefore describe a multidisciplinary problem spanning robotics,
artificial intelligence, perception, dialogue, interaction design, and safety (Fong et al. 2003;
Goodrich et al. 2007).
These concerns make responsibility boundaries important. Dialogue management must
preserve turn and participant context. Perception must retain the provenance and freshness
of observations. Planning must operate over capabilities the robot can actually invoke, while
execution must reject unsupported or unsafe work. A user-facing response must remain
connected to the evidence produced by these layers. The architecture studied in this thesis
treats those responsibilities as collaborating components rather than one end-to-end language
policy.
2.2 ROS 2 for Distributed Robot Software
The Robot Operating System (ROS) provides the communication infrastructure used to
distribute these responsibilities across robot software. Nodes exchange data through typed
topics, services, and actions. Topics carry streamed observations and state, services handle
bounded request-response operations, and actions support long-running or cancellable work
with feedback. ROS 2 extends this model with middleware and deployment mechanisms for
distributed robotic systems (Quigley et al. 2009; Macenski et al. 2022).
This communication model does not itself define HRI semantics or guarantee correct execution.
Its value for this thesis lies in the ability to assign ownership to observable interfaces. Language

models can remain at dialogue and task-planning boundaries, while robot actions, knowledge
queries, lifecycle state, and feedback retain typed ROS 2 owners. The same separation

2.3 Reusable HRI Representations and Interfaces
Reusable HRI systems require consistent representations of people and their observable signals.
Mohamed et al. (2020) identify the absence of a standard human representation as a barrier to
composing perception and interaction pipelines. Their ROS4HRI proposal defines conventions
for bodies, faces, voices, groups, and related social signals. This thesis uses those conventions
and compatible packages at the public person and speech boundaries; it does not treat
ROS4HRI as the planning or execution framework of the system.
In a modular HRI system, information passes through several levels of abstraction. Sensor and
speech streams first become tracked observations. Tracking and semantic sources associate
those observations with people, objects, and interaction groups. A knowledge representation
then exposes bounded entities and relations to dialogue and planning. User intent and
grounded roles become candidate skill plans, and admitted plans become typed robot actions
whose results feed the knowledge and dialogue paths. Each transition removes detail, so it
must preserve the identifiers, roles, freshness, and provenance required by the next layer.
An error at one transition can otherwise appear as a prompt, planning, or execution failure
downstream.
2.4 The NAO Robot Platform
The physical platform used in this thesis is NAO, a programmable humanoid robot originally
developed by Aldebaran Robotics. Gouaillier et al. (2008) describe NAO as an autonomous
humanoid platform for research, education, and RoboCup experimentation. Its speech
capabilities, articulated body, camera, and established academic use make it suitable for
studying spoken interaction, person and object grounding, gaze-oriented actions, motion, and
feedback-driven task execution. NAO is the embodied platform in the evaluated system, while
higher-level components depend on reviewed skills and ROS 2 interfaces rather than direct
model-generated NAOqi calls.
2.5 LLMs as High-Level Planners
Large language models have been studied as high-level planners because they encode procedural
and semantic knowledge that can be elicited from natural-language instructions. Huang et al.
(2022b) show that language models can decompose embodied tasks into action-like steps,
while also exposing the difficulty of mapping unconstrained language to admissible actions. A

linguistically reasonable plan can remain impossible, unsafe, or unsupported by the available
robot.
Several approaches ground model proposals in a restricted action set. SayCan combines
language-model task knowledge with affordance scores from pretrained skills (Ahn et al. 2022).
Code as Policies generates policy code over a supplied API, allowing feedback loops and
spatial computations while remaining dependent on reviewed callable primitives (Liang et al.
2022). These approaches motivate the use of an explicit skill registry in this thesis. The
model plans over declared capabilities rather than arbitrary natural-language actions.
Reasoning-and-acting methods also show the value of external evidence. ReAct interleaves
language reasoning with actions that query tools or environments (Yao et al. 2022). Inner
Monologue incorporates perception, success detectors, and human feedback into embodied
planning (Huang et al. 2022c). Reflexion stores verbalised feedback as experience across trials
without modifying model weights (Shinn et al. 2023). The system in this thesis adopts a
narrower runtime loop: a planner emits a structured skill plan, an orchestrator validates
and dispatches it, skills return typed results, and subsequent planning or dialogue acts are
conditioned on that feedback.
Prompt instructions alone cannot guarantee valid skills, arguments, grounding, or execution
order. The implemented stack therefore combines model output with schema validation,
registry lookup, argument checks, lineage checks, and deterministic dispatch. The distinction
is between using an LLM as one task-reasoning component and allowing it to become the
controller of the robot.
2.6 Embodied Multimodal Models, VLMs, and VLAs
Vision-language models (VLMs) connect visual observations with language, while vision-
language-action models (VLAs) also map visual and language inputs to robot actions. BLIP-2
connects frozen vision encoders and language models through a lightweight transformer (Li
et al. 2023). LLaVA uses visual instruction tuning to support image-conditioned interaction
(Liu et al. 2023b). These models show how visual evidence can be transformed into language-
compatible representations without themselves defining a complete robot runtime.
Embodied multimodal models extend this direction. PaLM-E incorporates visual and continu-
ous sensor inputs and evaluates them on embodied tasks (Driess et al. 2023). RT-2 represents
robot actions as tokens and co-trains on web and robot data (Brohan et al. 2023). OpenVLA
provides an open-source VLA trained on a broad collection of robot demonstrations (Kim
et al. 2024). This thesis does not train an end-to-end VLA policy. Visual and knowledge
evidence instead become compact symbolic context before reaching the dialogue and planning
components. Richer spatial or multimodal grounding remains a future extension.
6

2.7 Symbolic State and Knowledge Bases
High-level robot reasoning requires a representation of the world. Raw perception streams are
detailed and transient, while unstructured language summaries may omit identity, freshness,
or relation information. A symbolic knowledge base (KB) provides one bridge between these
representations. KnowRob represents objects, actions, environments, and task knowledge for
robot reasoning (Tenorth et al. 2013; Tenorth et al. 2017). RoboBrain similarly combines
multiple knowledge sources to support grounding, perception, and planning (Saxena et al.
2014). Other work studies how interaction can help a robot acquire environmental knowledge
and resolve referring expressions (Bastianelli et al. 2013; Grassi et al. 2021).
The implementation follows this principle at bounded scale. Detector-derived observations,
person state, and selected KB statements are projected into compact grounded context. The
projection retains stable handles, semantic type, interaction role, visibility, and a bounded
relation set. Dialogue and planning can use those entities without ingesting unrestricted
detector payloads or RDF rows. Skills may still query fresh source evidence at execution time.
This separation limits prompt growth and prevents planning-time state from being presented
as proof of a later physical effect.
2.8 Execution Monitoring, Replanning, and Clarification
Robot planning cannot stop at plan generation. Actions fail, targets disappear, a user may
change the goal, and safe continuation may require clarification. Feedback-oriented language-
agent and embodied-planning work supports this closed-loop view (Huang et al. 2022c; Yao
et al. 2022; Shinn et al. 2023).
The evaluated stack treats execution feedback as a typed runtime contract. A failed or
ambiguous result can stop dispatch, request user input, or return control to the planning
component. The orchestrator does not create replacement plans, and the planner does not
execute skills. Clarification follows the same ownership path: the planning component emits
a structured communication request and the dialogue component realises the user-facing
utterance. This keeps recovery observable and avoids inventing missing world facts.
2.9 Literature Gap
Prior work establishes the main ingredients separately. Language models can decompose tasks
into action-like sequences (Huang et al. 2022b); affordance and API grounding can restrict
proposals to available capabilities (Ahn et al. 2022; Liang et al. 2022); multimodal systems
can connect vision, language, and action (Driess et al. 2023; Brohan et al. 2023; Kim et al.
2024); symbolic systems can support robot knowledge and grounding (Tenorth et al. 2013;
Saxena et al. 2014); and feedback can condition later reasoning (Huang et al. 2022c; Yao et al.
2022).
7

The gap addressed here is the integration and validation of these responsibilities within one
modular architecture. The contribution combines dialogue lifecycle, semantic route selection,
compact symbolic grounding, skill-based planning, deterministic admission and dispatch, typed
execution feedback, and evidence-based reporting. It occupies a middle position between
fixed intent-to-skill mappings and end-to-end language or VLA policies. The architecture can
express composite tasks and react to feedback, while exposing interfaces that permit each
transition to be inspected independently.

3.4 Runtime Flow
During a normal turn, the dialogue manager receives the user’s request and passes it to the
language component together with the relevant conversation state. The language component
also receives a compact description of the current world. It then selects one of three outcomes:
ordinary dialogue, a knowledge answer, or an execution request. Dialogue and knowledge
questions return directly to the dialogue manager and do not activate the planner.
For an execution request, the extracted goal and grounded entities are passed to the planner.
The planner selects from the available skills and proposes an ordered plan. The orchestrator
checks that the plan contains supported skills and valid arguments before invoking anything
on the robot.
The orchestrator then executes the accepted steps in order. Each skill returns a result based
on current robot or world evidence. These results allow the orchestrator to complete the
plan, stop truthfully, or return feedback for replanning or clarification. The dialogue manager
communicates the final outcome to the user. Figure 3.2 summarises this flow.

4.2.1dialogue_manager
dialogue_manager is the ROS 2 lifecycle component responsible for multimodal communi-
cation between the robot and its interlocutors. Following the upstream package design1, it
preserves chronological conversation histories for active interlocutors and associates incoming
hri_msgs/LiveSpeech streams with the relevant dialogue state. The node invokes the chat-
bot through DialogueInteraction, exposes the high-level/skill/chat, /skill/ask, and
/skill/say interfaces, and realises final user-facing expressions through the configured Say
sub-skill.
This ownership boundary is also a runtime constraint. Planning and execution components
publish structured communication requests through the designated dialogue interfaces, while
dialogue_manager retains responsibility for the final utterance and its delivery through the
speech backend.
Planner dialogue acts return through the same boundary. planner_llm publishes struc-
tured acknowledgement, clarification, failure, or completion acts on/planner/dialogue_act;
nao_orchestrator relays the admitted act on/nao_orchestrator/planner_dialogue_act;
and the dialogue layer selects the final wording and speech mechanism. This arrangement
provides one user-facing speech authority for each semantic event and makes duplicate
acknowledgements or completion reports observable during validation.
4.2.2chatbot_llm
chatbot_llm is a lifecycle-managed semantic-routing node built on the existing chatbot
scaffold. Its turn callback receives aDialogueInteraction request and the conversation
history assembled bydialogue_manager. Before response generation or planner handoff, it
queries the KB throughkb_skills, reads the most recent/scene/summary, and passes both
sources through thePlannerHandoff projection helper. The resulting grounded_context,
defined by Contract 2, contains a bounded set of entities, locations, and task-relevant relations
instead of raw detector streams or unrestricted KB rows. This projection informs dialogue
and knowledge answers on every user turn and is attached to Contract 3 when the selected
route produces a planner request.

• A dialogue route covers conversational and task-discussion turns and does not publish
planner work.
• A knowledge_query route answers from current symbolic evidence without causing
a physical action. For example, “what can you see?” can use the current grounded
context, whereas “scan and tell me what you see” requests fresh sensing and is eligible
for execution.
• An execution route constructs planner-facing metadata such as goal text, request kind,
normalised intent hints, and target KB entities. It publishes a candidate request to

4.2.4 Goal-Keyed Planning-State Helper
The goal-keyed planning-state helper maintains planning state under an activegoal_id.
Each accepted plan carries aplan_id and monotonically meaningfulplan_version; stable
step_id values allow a newer plan version to preserve completed work when replanning
continues the same goal. Feedback from an older or unrelated version is rejected before it
can alter the current goal state. If a replacement plan is required,planner_llmcreates and
validates it under a newer version. Execution supervision and step dispatch remain within
nao_orchestrator; the helper maintains planning order and lineage across these transitions.

4.3 World State and Scene Grounding
The perception path has parallel people and object branches. The person branch reuses
hri_face_detect_yunet, hri_person_manager, and the ROS4HRI person interfaces to
provide identities, tracked-person topics, and frame-qualified state (Mohamed et al. 2020).
The object branch usesnao_scene_grounding to convert detector output into a provider-
independent scene representation. Its custom YOLO detector and restricted fruit-and-
vegetable training set were developed within the EMOROBCARE project and are reused here
rather than introduced as contributions of this thesis (Cooper et al. 2026). The reused detector
is integrated with the NAO camera stream through ROS; its configured demonstration classes
are blueberry, corn, pear, tomato, and zucchini. Detector backends are normalised into one
internal observation shape so that downstream components do not depend on the detector
message type.
The grounding node filters object detections by confidence and an explicit label allowlist,
maps labels to KB classes, and maintains local identifiers using tracker IDs or bounded
image-plane matching. Without a tracker ID, the default matcher requires the same label,
KB class, and detector source, a displacement of at most 64 pixels, and an observation age
of at most two seconds. It publishes/scene/summary and writes accepted observations as
transient KnowledgeCore statements through/kb/revise. Under the default configuration,
KB observations have a four-second lifespan and are refreshed no more often than once per
second; a local object is removed from the current scene summary after 4.5 seconds without a
new detection. An object marked visible ingrounded_context is therefore present in this

5.1 Validation Scope
The evaluation tests whether the designed system works from a user request to a correct
response or robot action. A case passes only when the request is interpreted correctly, the
relevant world information is used, any required plan contains suitable skills in the correct
order, the orchestrator executes only the accepted plan, and the final response agrees with
the observed outcome.
The unit of analysis is one user request under a fixed software, model, world-state, and skill
configuration. Before an independent case, the declared scene and KB fixture is reset or
verified; state is preserved only for an explicitly multi-turn or stateful sequence. The main
questionnaire is entered through the ROSrqt chat interface, which provides repeatable text
input to the same dialogue component used by the integrated system. Robot speech output is
checked separately when it is part of the case.

5.9 Threats to Validity
The fixed questionnaire covers only a bounded part of natural language, and repeated
development against known prompts may improve performance specifically on those prompts.
The robustness set reduces this risk through paraphrase and repetition but does not estimate
performance over unrestricted instructions. Controlled people, objects, and relations also
simplify the grounding problem compared with an uncontrolled environment.
Language-model output may vary even when the request, KB entities, prompt, and skill
registry are unchanged. A single pass can therefore overstate reliability, while comparisons
across different models or backend settings can confound model quality with configuration
changes. The methodology addresses this by retaining all repetitions, separating configuration
tuples, and limiting cross-model conclusions to matched evidence.
Deterministic simulated adapters make it possible to test the same planner, orchestrator,
feedback, and reporting logic independently of the robot platform. This reduces platform
dependence and supports reuse through equivalent skill adapters. Simulated results do not
measure physical navigation, grasping, manipulation, or safety. Physical observations in this
thesis are limited to the capabilities exercised on NAO, while the software-level conclusions
apply to platforms that provide equivalent skill and result interfaces.

Discussion
7.1 Model and Backend Variation
The primary result and the matched supplement answer different questions. The 64/72
Qwen3-VL result qualifies one frozen configuration across the complete primary matrix. The
191-record Ollama supplement instead asks which behaviours remain stable when the model
changes under a largely fixed semantic runtime. Keeping these denominators separate prevents
a broad comparison from weakening the evidential role of the primary qualification.
The matched results show both stability and variation. Every model passed the environment
profile, and both Gemma variants reproduced complete stateful-KB and controlled multi-
step results. At the same time, the profile rates differed, particularly in multi-capability
planning, KB wording, target selection, clarification, and closure. The architecture is therefore
model-agnostic at the interface level but not model-invariant at the semantic-output level.
7.2 Contract Containment and Qualification
The strongest architectural finding is containment rather than uniformly correct generation.
Typed requests, the reviewed skill registry, deterministic plan validation, execution lineage, and
evidence-based reporting made route, target, plan, clarification, and closure errors observable.
In the primary multi-capability profile, two incomplete plans were rejected before dispatch.
This prevented partial execution from being reported as completion, although the user requests
still failed.
The primary questionnaire exposes the same distinction. The red-cup update reached Knowl-
edgeCore but violated the knowledge-versus-execution route boundary. The kitchen request
stopped safely but lost the location and human-recipient roles. Safe non-dispatch is preferable
to executing an invalid plan, but it is not task success. A qualification procedure must
therefore score semantic route and role preservation as well as runtime safety.
The seven shared supplementary failures are useful because they limit simple model-quality

explanations. Their concentration in object-grounded picking, demanding composition,
recipient clarification, and recovery identifies boundaries that merit further instrumentation.
Shared outcomes identify behaviours that require more discriminating tests; model-specific
outcomes remain evidence of semantic variance.
7.3 Grounding, Roles, and Target Sets
The controlled environment and stateful KB results show that compact symbolic grounding
can preserve people, objects, locations, support relations, attributes, and post-action state
across the implemented interfaces. The cross-model environment result further supports
stable grounding transport during the matched runtime comparison. For the Gemma variants,
complete stateful-KB results also show that KnowledgeCore transport and the principal
query/revision paths remained available.
Correct source context is nevertheless different from correct model-authored use of that
context. A model can receive the relevant entities and still omit a member of an “all objects”
set, assign a location to a recipient field, or omit required grounded terms from speech.
Nemotron’s KB cases illustrate the last distinction: the evidence supports wording omissions,
not failure of the underlying KB revision transport.
Validation must consequently follow the entity set across request, grounding, target selection,
admitted plan, execution, postcondition, and report. Role constraints are equally impor-
tant: people remain recipients or human targets, locations remain anchors, and objects
remain manipulable entities. Grounded context supports these decisions but does not replace
deterministic comparison of the selected and required sets.
7.4 Failure Handling, Physical Claims, and Portability
The failure evidence is intentionally asymmetric. Beginning-stage navigation demonstrated a
complete stop, versioned replan, target preservation, revised execution, and closure. Middle-
stage pick evidence showed that completed work and lineage can survive a revised plan. Later
blocked-delivery and missing-object observations showed that replanning can occur without
reliable terminal closure. End-stage evidence therefore concerns final reporting and truthful
completion, not a dedicated symmetric last-skill injection.
Deterministic simulated adapters were essential for placing known outcomes at specific skill
calls while retaining the robot-facing result contract. Their evidence supports software
claims about admission, dispatch, replanning, postconditions, and reporting. Robot-facing
posture, head-motion and gaze, and text-to-speech adapters were exercised during integration,
providing an initial bridge from planned steps to observable NAO behaviour. These checks
were not a repeated physical-performance evaluation and do not establish navigation accuracy,
grasp reliability, manipulation success, perception accuracy, or human safety.

Limitations and Future Work
8.1 Current Limitations
The evaluation is scenario-based. The primary evidence index and fixed questionnaire provide
bounded evidence over known prompts, fixtures, skills, and failure policies rather than
exhaustive coverage of natural language or world states. Repeated development against these
prompts may also improve them more than unseen requests.
Only Qwen3-VL completed the full primary qualification. The separate three-model Ol-
lama supplement uses one runtime test series per model and therefore supports descriptive
engineering comparison rather than statistical ranking.
Observability is incomplete for a small part of the supplementary evidence. Not-scored and
unresolved timeout rows were excluded from strict denominators because they do not provide
a complete correlated outcome.
The failure-position evidence is asymmetric. Controlled beginning- and middle-stage failures
were retained, whereas end-stage evidence concerns missing final action or report closure
rather than a dedicated last-skill injection. The results therefore support the observed recovery
patterns but not a balanced comparison across every possible failure position.
Most execution evidence uses deterministic simulated skill adapters and controlled KB fixtures.
Posture transitions, head motion and gaze, and text-to-speech were exercised through robot-
facing adapters during integration, but not with the repeated trial matrix used for the
software results. The evidence therefore supports software-contract, replan, and symbolic-
state conclusions but not physical navigation, grasping, manipulation, perception accuracy, or
human safety. Object detection was disabled in the principal semantic profile, so detector
performance remains outside the primary result.

8.2 Future Work
8.2.1 Model Qualification and Semantic Guards
Parameter-efficient post-training, such as LoRA, could target exact role preservation, universal-
set coverage, valid structured output, clarification wording, plan completeness, and truthful
terminal closure. Training and evaluation sets should be separated by task template, entity
names, and paraphrase family. Any adapted or replacement model must repeat the complete
primary qualification and the sensitive matched cases rather than inherit qualification from
endpoint compatibility.
Deterministic guards should also compare requested and selected cardinalities, enforce ob-
ject/person/location role types, require every admitted composite capability to appear in the
plan, and verify report coverage against attempted targets. Knowledge-only mutations should
bypass the robot-action planner by construction. Terminal closure should become an explicit
orchestrator obligation with a deterministic failure reason when the language path cannot
formulate the required response.
8.2.2 Embodied Validation and Portability
A separate physical evaluation should extend the existing posture, head-motion, gaze, and
speech checks with direct observations, repeated trial counts, and any supported navigation or
manipulation adapters. A detector-enabled profile should test freshness, identity continuity,
ambiguity, and disagreement between perception and authored KB facts. These results must
remain separate from deterministic simulated-skill evidence.
Portability should be evaluated by implementing the same reviewed contracts on a second
ROS-compatible platform. Reuse would be demonstrated only if the alternative adapters
provide equivalent typed evidence and pass the same software qualification without changing
planner-facing semantics.
8.2.3 Evidence and Reproducibility
Future evaluations should retain one immutable runtime tuple, machine-readable manifests,
endpoint identity, startup logs, raw traces, derived indexes, and rendered summaries generated
from the same aggregate source. Sensitive cases should be repeated independently across
models before inferential comparisons are attempted. Automated checks should reject reports
when status semantics, denominators, shared-case intersections, or checksums disagree.
The primary 108-record index and the separate 191-record model supplement provide fixed
baselines for these extensions. New evaluations should be added as separately identified
evidence scopes rather than overwriting either dataset.

Chapter 9
Conclusion
This thesis presented the design, integration, and validation of a modular LLM-based inter-
active architecture implemented with the NAO robot. The contribution combines dialogue
routing, compact symbolic grounding, structured planning, a reviewed skill registry, determin-
istic orchestration, typed execution feedback, skill adapters, and evidence-based reporting.
The principal Qwen3-VL qualification produced 64 passes and eight non-passes across 72
records, and the 22-case end-to-end questionnaire produced 20 passes and two failures. The
separate 191-record comparison showed that the same interfaces could be exercised with three
Ollama-hosted models while semantic behaviour varied in composition, grounded selection,
clarification, and terminal closure. All three supplementary models preserved the environment
profile, while their remaining results confirmed that interface compatibility does not imply
equivalent semantic or planning behaviour.
The architecture demonstrated complete beginning-stage recovery in the fail-once navigation
case and preserved completed work during a middle-stage pick replan. It also rejected
incomplete composite plans before execution. These findings show that generated proposals can
be constrained by deterministic interfaces and that model errors need not become unrestricted
robot actions. They do not show that every task or recovery completes successfully, particularly
when target coverage, roles, clarification, or final reporting is incomplete.
The research aim is therefore supported within the tested scope. Language-model dialogue and
planning can participate in an integrated robot architecture when their outputs are grounded,
validated against reviewed skills, dispatched deterministically, and checked against typed
feedback and symbolic postconditions. These boundaries do not eliminate non-determinism.
They make it observable, restrict its execution authority, and provide a repeatable qualification
procedure for each model and runtime configuration.
